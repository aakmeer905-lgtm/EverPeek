About Ever Peak

Mountains are more than height

They are landforms, ecosystems, cultural landscapes and places where exploration, science and human endurance meet.

What is mountaineering?

Mountaineering is the activity of travelling and climbing in mountainous terrain. Depending on the route, it can involve hiking, scrambling, rock climbing, snow and ice climbing, route finding, navigation, glacier travel and expedition skills.

A high summit is not automatically a difficult summit. Difficulty depends on terrain, weather, altitude, route, season, technical climbing and the climber's preparation.

  • Physical endurance and altitude awareness
  • Navigation, route planning and weather decisions
  • Rock, snow, ice and glacier techniques where required
  • Respect for local communities and fragile mountain environments

Mountains around the world

Mountain systems occur on every continent and even beneath the oceans. There is no universally accepted global definition that gives one official total number of mountains.

Why mountains matter

UNESCO notes that mountain regions cover about a quarter of Earth's land surface and support major biodiversity, water and climate functions.

“Discovered” is not a simple number

Modern mapping has identified enormous numbers of peaks and landforms, but “discovered” can mean mapped, named, surveyed, climbed or scientifically studied. These are different things, so a reliable worldwide discovered/undiscovered percentage does not exist.

Mountain Facts

Numbers you can actually verify

Instead of inventing a worldwide mountain count, this page uses well-established categories and clearly labels estimates or classification limits.

No official world total

There is no single universally accepted number of mountains on Earth because the boundary between a mountain, peak, summit, ridge and hill depends on definitions and mapping criteria.

14 classic eight-thousanders

The UIAA recognizes the classic list of 14 principal peaks above 8,000 metres in the Himalaya and Karakoram.

Everest has thousands of climbers

An RGS educational resource states that approximately 3,000 people had climbed Everest since the 1953 first ascent. The number changes as new ascents occur.

Mountains are still studied

Exploration did not end when a summit was first reached. Scientists continue to study glaciers, climate, geology, biodiversity, hazards and changing mountain environments.

Altitude changes the challenge

At extreme altitude, cold, wind, low oxygen, dehydration, fatigue and rapidly changing weather can turn a technically simple section into a serious hazard.

Mountains support life

Mountain regions provide habitats, water resources and ecosystem services and are important to communities far beyond the mountains themselves.

The Classic 14

Eight-Thousanders

These are the 14 principal 8,000 m peaks recognized by the UIAA. A separate debate exists over subsidiary summits and what should count as an independent mountain.

Timeline

Important moments in mountaineering history

From early Alpine exploration to the great Himalayan expeditions.

1786

Mont Blanc first recorded ascent

Jacques Balmat and Dr Michel Paccard reached the summit of Mont Blanc on 8 August 1786, an event widely associated with the beginning of modern mountaineering.

1850s

The Golden Age of Alpinism

Many major Alpine summits were climbed during the 19th century, while route-finding, guiding and climbing techniques developed rapidly.

1921–1953

Everest reconnaissance and expeditions

Multiple British-led expeditions investigated routes on Everest. The 1951 reconnaissance identified a practical route through the Western Cwm toward the South Col.

1953

Everest summit reached

Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay reached the summit on 29 May 1953 during John Hunt's expedition—the first successful ascent of Everest.

1950–1964

The 14 eight-thousanders era

The first ascents of the classic 14 peaks occurred between 1950 and 1964, beginning with Annapurna I and ending with Shishapangma.

1986

Messner completes the classic 14

Reinhold Messner became the first climber to complete the classic 14 eight-thousanders, a landmark achievement recognized by the UIAA.

Today

Climbing meets science and conservation

Modern expeditions may combine climbing with glacier research, biodiversity studies, mapping, weather observation, conservation and responsible mountain tourism.

Safety & Reality

What can go wrong on a mountain?

Mountaineering is not just about reaching the summit. Good decisions, preparation and turning around when conditions are unsafe are part of the sport.

Weather

High winds, storms, whiteouts and sudden temperature changes can make routes dangerous.

Avalanches

Snow instability can create rapidly changing hazards on steep slopes and mountain passes.

Altitude

Thin air can cause altitude illness and severe fatigue. Acclimatization and medical awareness matter.

Cold injuries

Frostbite and hypothermia are serious risks in prolonged exposure to cold, wind and wet conditions.

Navigation

Glaciers, ridges and changing snow cover can make route-finding difficult even for experienced climbers.

Fatigue

Long approaches, heavy packs, altitude and poor sleep can reduce judgement and coordination.

Falls

Rockfall, slips and falls are major hazards on steep rock, snow and mixed terrain.

Decision making

The safest expedition is not always the one that reaches the summit. Turning back can be the correct outcome.

Important clarification

Discovered vs. undiscovered mountains

This section is deliberately accurate rather than giving a made-up number.

What we know

Thousands of named and mapped mountains, peaks and summits are documented in national maps, satellite datasets and geographic databases. But the word “mountain” is not defined identically everywhere.

What we cannot honestly claim

There is no authoritative worldwide figure saying “X mountains are discovered and Y are undiscovered.” Remote regions can still contain unmapped or poorly studied features, and new mapping can split or merge named peaks.

The cleanest measurable category

For a school/project website, the classic 14 eight-thousanders are a strong measurable category: they are the 14 principal peaks above 8,000 m recognized by the UIAA.

Discovery continues

Modern exploration includes new routes, previously unclimbed summits, scientific surveys, glacier measurements, biodiversity work and detailed mapping—not simply finding a mountain that nobody knew existed.
