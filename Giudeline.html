Start Here

Prepare Before You Climb

Mountaineering requires planning, knowledge, suitable equipment and respect for changing mountain conditions. No gadget can replace proper training or sound decisions.

Navigation

Use maps, a compass and suitable GPS devices. Learn how to navigate even when batteries fail or electronic signals are unavailable.

Personal Safety

Carry appropriate protective equipment, first-aid supplies, emergency shelter and reliable lighting for your planned route.

Communication

Choose a communication method suitable for the area. Mobile coverage may be limited or completely unavailable in remote mountains.

Weather Awareness

Check forecasts, wind, temperature, precipitation and avalanche information where relevant. Conditions can change quickly at altitude.

From History to Today

Traditional Mountaineering Tools

Before modern satellite devices and digital navigation, mountaineers relied on simple equipment, observation, experience and teamwork. Some traditional tools remain useful today, while others have been replaced or improved.

Paper Maps and Compass

Paper maps and magnetic compasses were central navigation tools long before consumer GPS became common. They remain valuable backups because they do not depend on battery power. Mountaineers should practise bearings, map orientation and route planning.

Ice Axes and Metal Crampons

Ice axes and crampons evolved as mountaineers travelled across snow and ice. Modern designs vary for walking, glacier travel, steep ice and technical climbing. Using the wrong design or technique can cause serious injury.

Lanterns and Early Torches

Older expeditions used candles, lanterns and early torches for illumination. Modern LED headlamps are lighter and more convenient, but batteries can still fail. Always plan for backup lighting and avoid open flames inside tents.

Canvas Packs and Wool Clothing

Traditional packs and wool clothing supported earlier journeys. Modern packs, synthetic layers and technical waterproof fabrics can improve comfort and organisation, but must still be selected for the weather and activity.

Important Lesson

Technology changes, but the basic principles remain: know your route, prepare for bad weather, carry suitable equipment, understand its limitations and make conservative decisions when conditions deteriorate.

Smart Equipment

Guidelines for Modern Gadgets

Modern devices can make a trip easier to monitor and organise. They work best when the user understands their limits and carries appropriate backup equipment.

GPS and Navigation Devices

Prepare your route before leaving.

  • Download maps and planned routes before the trip.
  • Learn how to read coordinates and elevation information.
  • Carry a suitable paper map and compass as backups.
  • Check battery levels and power-saving settings.
  • Do not assume that GPS reception means mobile service is available.
  • Confirm your location using terrain features when possible.

Satellite Messengers and PLBs

Understand emergency communication.

  • Check whether the service operates in your destination.
  • Activate subscriptions and register devices as required.
  • Read the manufacturer's instructions for SOS activation.
  • Keep the device accessible, charged and protected.
  • Understand the difference between messaging devices and distress beacons.
  • Do not rely on a phone's emergency features without verifying their coverage and availability.

GPS Watches and Health Monitors

Use data as supporting information.

  • Charge the device and learn the main controls beforehand.
  • Understand battery life in the mode you plan to use.
  • Check whether maps and navigation work offline.
  • Protect the device from impact, moisture and cold.
  • Do not use one health reading to decide whether it is safe to continue climbing.
  • Take unusual symptoms seriously even when a device appears normal.

Batteries and Power Banks

Keep essential equipment powered.

  • Charge devices fully before departure.
  • Check cable and connector compatibility.
  • Keep batteries insulated from extreme cold.
  • Follow manufacturer advice for charging and storage temperatures.
  • Protect batteries from water, crushing and short circuits.
  • Reserve power for navigation and emergency communication.
Essential Knowledge

Complete Mountain Safety Guidelines

Follow these general principles when planning a mountain journey. The exact requirements depend on the route, season, altitude, weather, local regulations and the experience of your group.

01

Research the Route and Plan the Trip

Good planning begins before you reach the mountain.

  • Research route distance, elevation gain, terrain and estimated time.
  • Study the difficulty and technical requirements of the route.
  • Check recent trail reports, closures, permits and local regulations.
  • Check official weather forecasts and relevant avalanche bulletins.
  • Choose a route suitable for the least experienced member of your group.
  • Set a turnaround time and be prepared to abandon the summit attempt.
  • Tell a reliable person your route, expected return time and emergency plan.
02

Inspect All Equipment Before Departure

Never assume old or unused equipment is safe.

  • Inspect helmets for damage and follow manufacturer replacement guidance.
  • Check ropes, harnesses, carabiners and other climbing equipment for damage and wear.
  • Do not use equipment that has been involved in a significant fall or impact unless the manufacturer or a qualified professional confirms it remains safe.
  • Check headlamp function and carry a backup light.
  • Test GPS, compass, radios and communication devices before departure.
  • Check tent poles, sleeping bags, footwear, clothing and waterproof layers.
  • Make sure every team member understands how their essential equipment works.

Equipment Warning

Never experiment with unfamiliar technical climbing equipment on a dangerous route. Learn from a qualified instructor and follow the equipment manufacturer's instructions.

03

Wear the Right Clothing and Footwear

Protect yourself from cold, wind, rain and sun.

  • Use a layering system suitable for expected conditions.
  • Carry insulating and waterproof clothing when needed.
  • Wear footwear appropriate for the terrain and planned activity.
  • Use gloves, a hat and sun protection as conditions require.
  • Keep spare dry layers protected from rain and snow.
  • Avoid clothing choices that leave you vulnerable to wind chill or wet conditions.
  • Remember that conditions at the summit can be very different from the trailhead.
04

Use Navigation Equipment Correctly

Know where you are and where you are going.

  • Study the map before starting and identify important landmarks.
  • Check your position at sensible intervals rather than waiting until you are lost.
  • Use a compass and map if electronic navigation fails.
  • Do not follow an unfamiliar route simply because a GPS line appears on a screen.
  • Avoid shortcuts across cliffs, unstable slopes, glaciers or other hazardous terrain.
  • If visibility deteriorates, stop in a safe place and reassess the route.
  • If you are uncertain about the route, turn back when doing so is safer.
05

Monitor Weather and Environmental Conditions

Mountain weather can change rapidly.

  • Check forecasts before departure and monitor updates when possible.
  • Watch for increasing wind, falling temperatures, thunder, heavy rain or snowfall.
  • Understand the local risks of lightning, flash floods, rockfall and avalanches.
  • Avoid exposed ridges during dangerous thunderstorms.
  • Do not enter avalanche terrain without the required training, equipment and current conditions assessment.
  • Do not cross swollen streams or unstable snow bridges.
  • Retreat early if conditions become unsafe.
06

Hydration, Food and Energy Management

Maintain energy throughout the journey.

  • Carry enough water for the expected duration and conditions.
  • Plan how you will obtain and safely treat additional water if needed.
  • Carry food that is suitable for the length and difficulty of the trip.
  • Eat and drink regularly instead of waiting until you feel exhausted.
  • Protect water and food from freezing when necessary.
  • Adjust your pace to the group, altitude, terrain and weather.
  • Rest when needed and avoid pushing through severe exhaustion.
07

Climbing Ropes, Harnesses and Protection

Technical climbing requires proper training.

  • Learn rope systems and belaying from a qualified climbing instructor.
  • Use climbing equipment designed for the specific activity.
  • Check knots, harness fit, connectors and belay systems according to established procedures.
  • Communicate clearly with climbing partners before movement and belaying.
  • Never assume a knot or attachment is correct without checking it.
  • Do not use improvised anchors or unfamiliar rescue systems without appropriate expertise.
  • Use helmets where rockfall, icefall or climbing impacts are possible.

Technical Climbing Warning

Reading a webpage is not a substitute for practical instruction. Ropework, glacier travel, crevasse rescue, avalanche rescue and technical ice climbing should be learned through qualified training.

08

Altitude Awareness

Take altitude-related symptoms seriously.

  • Ascend gradually according to recognised altitude-safety guidance.
  • Allow time for acclimatisation on high-altitude trips.
  • Learn the warning signs of altitude illness before departure.
  • Headache, nausea, dizziness or unusual fatigue after gaining altitude should not be ignored.
  • Confusion, difficulty walking, severe breathlessness or worsening symptoms require urgent action.
  • Do not continue ascending when altitude illness is suspected.
  • Follow appropriate medical guidance; descent may be urgently necessary in serious cases.

Health and Altitude Warning

Severe symptoms at altitude can be life-threatening. Seek urgent professional assistance and follow local emergency procedures. Do not rely on a smartwatch or a single oxygen-saturation reading to rule out danger.

09

Camp Safely in Mountain Environments

Choose a suitable site and prepare for the night.

  • Choose a campsite away from rockfall paths, avalanche runouts and flood-prone areas.
  • Avoid exposed locations when dangerous winds or lightning are forecast.
  • Secure tents according to the terrain and expected weather.
  • Keep cooking equipment and flames outside tents and other enclosed spaces.
  • Never operate fuel-burning stoves or heaters inside enclosed shelters because carbon monoxide can be fatal.
  • Keep essential equipment dry, organised and accessible.
  • Follow local rules for camping, fires, waste and wildlife.
10

Leave No Trace and Protect Nature

Keep mountains clean for future visitors.

  • Carry out rubbish, food wrappers and used batteries.
  • Use established trails and campsites where appropriate.
  • Do not damage plants, disturb wildlife or remove natural objects.
  • Respect local communities, cultural sites and protected areas.
  • Use toilets and waste-disposal systems according to local guidance.
  • Keep noise low and respect other climbers and hikers.
  • Follow applicable conservation rules and fire restrictions.

When Things Go Wrong

Mountain Emergency Guidelines

In an emergency, protect life first. Use the local emergency procedures and contact the relevant rescue service whenever possible.

1. Call for Help

Use the available local emergency number or a suitable satellite distress device. Give your location, situation, number of people and important hazards as clearly as possible.

2. Share Your Location

Provide coordinates or clear landmarks if you can identify them safely. Keep your communication device available and follow instructions from emergency responders.

3. Protect the Injured

Avoid additional hazards, provide appropriate first aid within your training and protect the injured person from cold, wind and wet conditions.

4. Avoid Risky Shortcuts

Do not attempt a dangerous descent or technical rescue beyond your ability. Moving may increase the danger. Follow trained rescuers' guidance.

5. Keep the Group Together

Where safe, keep the group organised and account for everyone. Avoid sending unprepared people away alone to seek help.

6. Make Conservative Decisions

If the weather, visibility, health or equipment makes the route unsafe, stop and reassess. Reaching the summit is never more important than returning safely.

Remember

Emergency procedures differ by country and region. Learn the local emergency number and rescue arrangements before your journey. A phone, GPS watch or satellite device cannot guarantee that rescue will be immediate.
