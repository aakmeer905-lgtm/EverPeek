History of Mountaineering

From ancient high-altitude journeys to the great Himalayan expeditions, the history of mountaineering is a story of exploration, science, courage, teamwork, technology and respect for the world's highest mountains.

The Beginning of Mountain Climbing

Mountaineering did not begin with one single person or one single mountain.

Humans have travelled through mountains for thousands of years for hunting, trade, migration, religion, exploration and scientific study. High mountains were also reached by local peoples long before European mountaineering became a formal activity.

Archaeological and historical evidence shows that people in the Andes reached very high summits centuries before modern mountaineering. Some high-altitude Inca ascents are known from archaeological discoveries.

Therefore, it is not historically accurate to name one person as the first human to climb a mountain. Modern mountaineering, however, developed gradually as an organized sport and scientific pursuit, especially in Europe during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

Mountaineering Timeline

Important events that shaped the history of mountain climbing.

Ancient Times

People Reach High Mountains

Long before modern mountaineering, people climbed mountains for survival, hunting, travel, religion, exploration and other purposes. High-altitude archaeological evidence in the Andes shows that pre-modern peoples reached extremely high summits.

1492

Mont Aiguille

In the European Alps, Antoine de Ville and his companions climbed Mont Aiguille in France. The ascent is often remembered as an important early European mountain-climbing event.

1786

First Ascent of Mont Blanc

Dr. Michel-Gabriel Paccard and Jacques Balmat reached the summit of Mont Blanc on August 8, 1786. The ascent became one of the defining events in the development of modern mountaineering.

1787

Scientific Mountaineering

Horace-Bénédict de Saussure climbed Mont Blanc the following year. Scientific observation, measurement and exploration became increasingly connected with mountain journeys.

1854–1865

The Golden Age of Alpine Climbing

The middle of the nineteenth century saw a rapid increase in difficult Alpine ascents. Climbers, guides and explorers developed new techniques for rock, snow and ice.

1857

The Alpine Club

The Alpine Club was founded in London. It became an important organization in the development and documentation of Alpine climbing.

1865

First Ascent of the Matterhorn

Edward Whymper and his team reached the summit of the Matterhorn on July 14, 1865. The descent was marked by a tragic accident in which four members of the party died.

Late 1800s

Exploration of the Himalaya

Surveying and geographical exploration expanded in the Himalaya. Maps and measurements improved and helped future expeditions understand routes to the world's highest mountains.

1921

First British Everest Reconnaissance

A British expedition explored Everest from the Tibetan side. George Mallory was among the climbers. The expedition helped establish possible routes toward the summit.

1924

Mallory and Irvine Disappear

George Mallory and Andrew Irvine disappeared during the 1924 Everest expedition. Whether they reached the summit before their deaths remains one of mountaineering's famous historical mysteries.

1950

First 8000-Metre Summit

Maurice Herzog and Louis Lachenal reached the summit of Annapurna I. Annapurna became the first mountain above 8,000 metres to be successfully climbed.

1953

Everest is Climbed

Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay reached the summit of Mount Everest on May 29, 1953, becoming the first confirmed climbers to stand on the world's highest summit.

1954

First Ascent of K2

Italian climbers Achille Compagnoni and Lino Lacedelli reached the summit of K2. K2 became the second 8,000-metre peak to be successfully climbed.

1950s–1960s

The 8000-Metre Race

Expeditions rapidly climbed the world's highest mountains. One after another, the major 8,000-metre peaks were reached, culminating in the first ascent of Shishapangma in 1964.

1975

Junko Tabei Reaches Everest

Japanese climber Junko Tabei became the first woman confirmed to reach the summit of Mount Everest.

1978

Everest Without Supplemental Oxygen

Reinhold Messner and Peter Habeler became the first climbers confirmed to reach Everest without supplemental oxygen.

1986

Messner Completes the 14 Eight-Thousanders

Reinhold Messner became the first person to climb all fourteen of the commonly recognized 8,000-metre peaks. The UIAA recognizes this achievement.

1990s

Commercial Everest Expeditions Grow

Commercial guiding became increasingly important on Everest. More climbers began attempting the mountain with professional guides, fixed ropes, high-altitude support and modern expedition logistics.

2000s

Technology Changes Climbing

Satellite communication, GPS, weather forecasting, lightweight equipment, modern fabrics, improved climbing hardware and better medical knowledge changed expedition planning and safety.

2021

First Winter Ascent of K2

A Nepali team reached the summit of K2 in winter on January 16, 2021. K2 had been the last of the commonly recognized fourteen 8,000-metre peaks without a successful winter ascent.

The Golden Age of Alpine Climbing

The nineteenth century transformed climbing from occasional exploration into an organized mountaineering culture.

Mountain Guides

Local mountain guides became essential partners for climbers. Their knowledge of glaciers, weather, terrain and routes was critical to successful expeditions.

Better Maps

Scientific surveys and improved maps allowed climbers to understand mountain ranges and plan increasingly ambitious routes.

Climbing Techniques

Ice axes, ropes, crampons, pitons and improved footwear gradually changed the way climbers moved through snow, ice and rock.

The Story of Mount Everest

Why Everest Became the World's Most Famous Mountain

Mount Everest became a major target of exploration after surveys established its extraordinary height. British expeditions attempted the mountain during the 1920s and continued exploring possible routes.

The 1924 disappearance of George Mallory and Andrew Irvine created a lasting mystery. Mallory had previously participated in the 1921 expedition, and the 1924 team came extremely high on the mountain.

After further attempts and changing political circumstances, Nepal's southern route became accessible to major expeditions. The British expedition of 1953 finally succeeded.

On May 29, 1953, Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay reached the summit. Their achievement became one of the defining moments in twentieth-century mountaineering.

People Who Shaped Mountaineering History

1786

Michel-Gabriel Paccard

French physician and mountaineer who, together with Jacques Balmat, made the first successful ascent of Mont Blanc.

1786

Jacques Balmat

Chamonix guide and mountaineer who accompanied Paccard during the first successful ascent of Mont Blanc.

1865

Edward Whymper

British mountaineer associated with the first ascent of the Matterhorn and the development of Alpine climbing history.

1920s

George Mallory

British mountaineer who participated in early Everest expeditions and disappeared high on Everest during the 1924 expedition.

1950

Maurice Herzog

Leader of the French Annapurna expedition that made the first successful ascent of an 8,000-metre peak.

1953

Edmund Hillary

New Zealand mountaineer who reached the summit of Everest with Tenzing Norgay on May 29, 1953.

1953

Tenzing Norgay

Sherpa mountaineer who reached the summit of Everest with Edmund Hillary in 1953. Sherpa climbers and mountain workers played a crucial role in Himalayan expeditions.

Junko Tabei

Japanese mountaineer who became the first woman to reach the summit of Mount Everest.

1978–1986

Reinhold Messner

Italian mountaineer who became the first person to climb all fourteen commonly recognized 8,000-metre peaks. He was also among the first to climb Everest without supplemental oxygen.

Women in Mountaineering History

A Growing Role in High-Altitude Climbing

Women have played an increasingly important role in mountaineering. During the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, women challenged the idea that serious mountain climbing was only for men.

In 1975, Junko Tabei became the first woman to reach the summit of Everest. Her achievement became an important milestone in the history of women in mountaineering.

Since then, women from many countries have completed difficult ascents on Everest, K2, the other 8,000-metre peaks, major Alpine faces and remote mountain ranges.

Modern Mountaineering

Today's mountaineering combines traditional skills with advanced technology and scientific knowledge.

Weather Forecasting

Modern expeditions use detailed weather forecasts, satellite information and mountain meteorology to choose safer climbing windows.

GPS & Communication

GPS devices, satellite phones, tracking systems and digital communication have transformed expedition planning and emergency response.

Mountain Medicine

Modern understanding of altitude illness, acclimatization, rescue systems and emergency medicine has improved preparation for high-altitude climbing.

Better Equipment

Modern crampons, ice axes, ropes, helmets, harnesses, tents, sleeping systems and technical clothing make today's expeditions very different from early climbing journeys.

Photography & Film

Cameras, drones and digital media allow expeditions to document mountains and share climbing experiences with people around the world.

Mountain Protection

Modern mountaineering increasingly recognizes the importance of protecting fragile mountain ecosystems, reducing waste and respecting local communities.

Mountaineering History in Pakistan

The Karakoram and the Great Mountains of Pakistan

Pakistan is home to some of the world's most famous high-altitude mountains, including K2, Nanga Parbat, Broad Peak, Gasherbrum I and Gasherbrum II.

The Karakoram became one of the most important areas in twentieth-century Himalayan and high-altitude exploration. Expeditions from different countries came to the region to explore and climb its huge peaks and technical routes.

Nanga Parbat was first climbed successfully in 1953 by Hermann Buhl. K2 was first climbed in 1954 by Achille Compagnoni and Lino Lacedelli. Broad Peak was climbed in 1957, while Gasherbrum I was first climbed in 1958.

Pakistan's mountain history also includes the essential contribution of local guides, porters and high-altitude workers, especially communities from the Karakoram and surrounding regions.

Was There a First Mountain Ever Discovered?

There is no reliable historical answer naming one mountain as the first mountain ever discovered by humans.

Mountains existed in the knowledge of local communities for thousands of years. People lived around them, travelled through them, hunted on them, worshipped them and sometimes climbed them long before modern maps or mountaineering records existed.

Important: "First discovered mountain" and "first recorded modern mountaineering ascent" are two different things.

The first successful ascent of Mont Blanc in 1786 is widely regarded as one of the major milestones in the development of modern mountaineering.

The Legacy of Mountaineering

The history of mountaineering is much more than a list of people reaching summits. It includes local mountain communities, guides, porters, scientists, surveyors, expedition leaders, climbers and rescue teams.

Every generation has changed the way mountains are understood and climbed. Early explorers relied on simple equipment and local knowledge. Later climbers introduced organized expeditions, improved maps, ropes, crampons, ice axes and climbing techniques. Modern climbers use advanced weather forecasting, satellite communication, lightweight equipment and medical knowledge.

At the same time, mountaineering history has taught an important lesson: a mountain is not simply an object to be conquered. Mountains are ecosystems, cultural landscapes and homes to communities. Modern mountaineering therefore also includes responsibility for safety, the environment and respect for local people.
